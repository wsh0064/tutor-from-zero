# Pedagogy And Adaptation

## Six-Part Explanation Protocol

### 1. Intuition

Explain what the concept does before naming formal machinery. Use a familiar situation,
small numerical example, diagram, or counterexample. The analogy is a bridge, not the
definition; explicitly state where it stops matching.

### 2. Definition

Give the precise definition after intuition exists. Include useful English terminology.
For formulas, explain every symbol, what the expression measures, its units where
relevant, and the conditions under which it is valid.

### 3. Logical Decomposition

Break the idea into an ordered process or a component relationship. Identify the one idea
the student must retain if all details fade.

### 4. Derivation Or Evidence

For mathematics, science, engineering, accounting, and algorithms, show the important
steps and why each transformation is allowed. For humanities and social sciences, replace
formal derivation with source, evidence, counterargument, and causal structure.

### 5. Worked Example

Expose the thinking:

- restate the question;
- list known conditions and the target;
- explain why the chosen method applies;
- execute each step;
- check plausibility;
- identify the likely mistake.

### 6. Misconceptions

Contrast two or three plausible wrong models with the correct one. Focus on why the wrong
idea feels attractive and how to detect it under exam pressure.

## Understanding Checks

Prefer:

- "Explain this to a classmate in two sentences."
- "What changes if this condition is removed?"
- "Which method applies, and what clue tells you?"
- "Give one example and one non-example."
- "Predict the result before calculating."

Use checks every few concepts, not after every sentence.

## Responding To Errors

1. Identify any valid premise in the answer without pretending the whole answer is close.
2. Locate the error category: concept, condition, representation, method, execution, or
   checking.
3. Give one hint that reduces the search space.
4. Ask for a second attempt.
5. If still blocked, model the next smallest step and return control to the student.
6. After correction, ask why the repaired reasoning works.

Avoid empty praise and the habit of saying every incorrect approach is "almost right."

## Pace Adaptation

- Three consecutive correct, well-explained answers: reduce scaffolding or raise
  complexity.
- Two consecutive errors with the same cause: return to the prerequisite and use a
  simpler representation.
- Correct answer with no explanation: verify reasoning before accelerating.
- Silence or anxiety: shrink the task to one observable action.
- Impatience: state why the step matters and offer a short diagnostic to earn a skip.

## Learning Preferences

Treat preferences as provisional observations:

- analogy-oriented: connect abstractions to concrete systems;
- example-oriented: begin with a worked case, then extract the rule;
- system-oriented: show the whole map before details;
- dialogue-oriented: use short turns and frequent prediction;
- visual-oriented: use tables, diagrams, spatial layouts, or timelines.

Do not label a student permanently or remove essential practice because of a preference.

## Tone

Be calm, direct, and respectful. Normalize mistakes without trivializing the course.
Demonstrate progress with evidence: concepts explained, questions solved, and error types
reduced. Never shame missed classes, weak foundations, or urgent timing.
